# Why the voice system did not produce a good chapter 2 (diagnosis, 2026-11-17 13:30). Read before any further story writing.

Written under the stop-loss rule. Chapter 2 dialogue has now failed in two conversations: two drafts patched from the old game lines, then four versions of the loft sample written from the Voice Studio settings (claude/story-ch02-sample-loft.md, claude/story-ch02-pitches.md). Tom, 13:18: "The only thing that it did was pull in the approved lines that we had created. It didn't actually use the character mix and tone settings and everything that we've done to uniquely invent a new conversation, to pull them all together in a unique and new way."

Do not write a fifth version by the same method.

## What was tried
1. Keep the game's lines, patch what Tom marks (rejected: old voice).
2. Voice Studio: per-character references, signature moves, sliders, rules, pinned lines, approved sample lines. Tom approved the voices and liked the single sample lines.
3. Write chapter 2 scenes from those settings (rejected: copied the approved lines; when copying was banned, the scene went flat).

## What is actually wrong
| Cause | Evidence |
|---|---|
| The Voice Studio's six test moments were chapter 2 situations (a hill, purple smog, a fish in a muffin pan, a lonely monster). So the lines Tom approved are ready-made chapter 2 lines, and the writer reused them. | Pitches reuse "I take naps on that hill", the leaning mug, the gummy worms, Nara dropping a thing on Tom's shoe. Tom: "I thought I was approving the style of humor, not the exact line." This was a design mistake in the Studio. |
| The method told the writer to match the approved and pinned lines. Copying is the surest way to match. | claude/voice-studio.md section 4, step 11. |
| Everything in the settings describes HOW a person talks. Nothing creates WHAT is funny about a scene: what each person wants in it, what gets in the way, how it escalates. Voice settings can stop an out-of-character line. They cannot invent a situation. | The pitches' "one thing that makes it funny" column only restates a move or an existing line. |
| A reference blend was turned into fixed move templates once, and after that only the templates were used. Templates repeat. | "Okay. So. I'm going to need..." and "There's a tab just for..." recur across pinned exchanges. Loft version 4 stretches one move (the list) over ten lines. |
| Each of Tom's marks became one more ban. With a dozen checks per line the writer plays safe: plain lines, or lines already known to pass. | Loft version 4 breaks no rule and is not funny. |

## What still holds (not wasted)
- The character facts, Never rules, firm rules and pair rules. They are the right check for "not how they talk".
- Tom's right and wrong marks with reasons. They are his taste on record. They belong with a judge, not in front of the writer.
- The finding that single reaction lines came out well in the Studio. The failure is at the level of the scene.

## What the next attempt must do differently
1. Scene first, voice second. For each scene decide what each person wants, what is in the way, and the one comic situation that follows from it, before any line is written. Tom reacts to these, not to lines.
2. The writer does not see example lines. It gets who the person is, the references, the mechanism of each move without its example, and the scene. The approved and rejected lines go only to a separate judge.
3. The judge scores candidates for funny and clear first, rules second, and rejects any line that repeats a stored example.
4. Replace the Studio's test moments with moments that are in no chapter, and treat the lines approved so far as taste data only, never as chapter lines.
5. One scene as the proof. If that scene is still flat, the limit is the writer, and the fallback is that Tom or a writing partner supplies the comic idea for each scene and Claude writes it up in voice.

claude/story-method-v2.md (written by the second conversation) already has points 2 and 3 in part. It lacks point 1, which is the main gap.
