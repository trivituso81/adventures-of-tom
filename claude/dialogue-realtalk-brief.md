## Why repeating would fail (diagnosis)
1. Both tries were line edits on top of the old script. The old script was written around a metaphor ("amber is warmth that got stuck", "give it somewhere to go"), so every edit inherited the metaphor and had characters explain it as if they already knew it.
2. The scenes skip the human steps. Real people who find a neighbor frozen in a yellow block are scared, ask what it is, touch it, knock on it, shout, try something dumb, and only then find out what works. The script goes straight to the answer.
3. The same habit is across Part 2 and in places in Part 1: aphorisms standing in for plain statements.
4. That conversation was long and soaked in the old script, which kept steering the wording.

## What to do differently (the method)
- Fresh conversation. Do NOT start from the old lines. For each scene, write from a blank page as a screenplay: what do these specific people see, feel, ask and try, in order.
- Pilot on chapter 12 ONLY. Give Tom the chapter 12 scenes as a readable script (one page, script layout, not a before and after table). Build nothing and ask him to record nothing until he says the pilot reads like real people. Only then do the rest of the game the same way, chapter by chapter, Part 2 first.
- Tom should not record more reworded story lines until the pilot is approved (recommended to him 22:25).

## Rules for every line
| Rule | Meaning |
|---|---|
| React first | Surprise, fear or confusion comes before any explanation. Somebody says the obvious thing ("What is that?", "Is he dead?", "Can't we melt it?"). |
| Nobody knows what they could not know | A character may only state a fact they just saw, touched, were told, or tested. No one explains the villain's magic from nowhere. |
| Find out by doing | The rule of a thing is discovered physically on screen (try it, fail, notice, try again), then said in plain words. |
| Plain words | Say the literal thing. "It melts when it gets hot." Not "warmth that got stuck" or "give the warmth somewhere to go". |
| Metaphor only as a joke or from the villain | If Vance or Marlowe speaks in riddles, a family member answers "What does that mean?" and gets, or works out, a plain answer. |
| Kids ask, adults admit | Grace and Rose ask the blunt questions. Adults are allowed to say "I don't know." |
| Keep the fun | Humor, inside jokes, catchphrases and character voice stay. Contractions. No em dashes. |
