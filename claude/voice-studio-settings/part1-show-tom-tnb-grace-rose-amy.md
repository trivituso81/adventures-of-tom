Rules for every line:
Each line answers the line before it. A question always gets an answer.
Shock or fear comes first. The joke comes after.
Say plainly what a thing is and what a person is doing. No shorthand and no joke that has to be decoded.
Jokes come from who the person is. Never from someone being stupid, mean or incompetent.
Nobody states their own personality, their feelings about the theme, or the moral.
No line explains its own joke. A mild wry remark is not a joke: cut it and end on the last good line.
Short. Nothing said twice. Nothing said that the action already shows.
Contractions always. No em dashes.
A child can read it. Nothing inappropriate. No talk of dying in front of a child.
Never invent a relative, living or dead. The real names Angelo and Debi never appear.
Nobody jokes about or doubts Boppy's stomach or health.
The family never says amber. It is an orange block or an orange cube.
Tom and TNB never keep count of each other's puns.
No banter that implies Tom or TNB cares about something more than the other.

CHARACTER: Tom
Who: The player. Uncle to Grace and Rose. Splits his life between Long Island and Los Angeles.
Background: Product director who works from home in Jefferson Park, Los Angeles. Flies Delta One back to Long Island to see his parents, his sister Amy and his nieces. Loves video games, rules, exact numbers, his sourdough starter and skincare. Analytical, caring and critical. Underneath, he is afraid the girls are growing up while he is away, and he also wants the life he is building in Los Angeles with TNB. He never says that out loud.
How they are funny: He takes small things very seriously, is polite to things that cannot hear him, overreacts, and then laughs at himself. Witty, likes a pun, likes to get a laugh out of people.
Sounds like a blend of: 60% Leslie Knope (Parks and Recreation), 40% David Rose (Schitt's Creek)
SIGNATURE MOVES
- The oddly specific compliment (from Leslie Knope (Parks and Recreation), which is 60% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Greets someone as you or my, then two sincere adjectives, then one unexpected and very specific noun. The noun is strange but the feeling is completely sincere. When: Greeting someone she adores, or cheering them on. Example: There she is! My loud, fearless little hedge raccoon. Delivery: Warm and delighted, building through the adjectives. The odd noun is said with pride, never as a joke. Limits: For Grace, Rose and Nara only. Never sarcastic. At most once a chapter.
- Already prepared for this (from Leslie Knope (Parks and Recreation), which is 60% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Reveals she already made a plan, list or document for this exact small situation, and names one absurdly specific part of it. When: Someone raises a problem or an outing. Example: I made a spreadsheet for this trip. There's a tab just for snacks. Delivery: Proud and matter of fact, as if everyone does this. The specific detail is tossed off lightly. Limits: The plan is about something small and domestic. He is never smug about it.
- The wonky dream (from Leslie Knope (Parks and Recreation), which is 60% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: Declares a dull, over-specific thing to be a universal thrill, in the form: every X dreams of Y. When: Offered something small and official. Example: Every uncle dreams of a lanyard with his own name on it. Delivery: Earnest and glowing, with no irony at all. Limits: Only about something Tom would truly love: rules, badges, labels, exact numbers.
- Polite fury (from Leslie Knope (Parks and Recreation), which is 60% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Anger said in formal, courteous words. The politeness goes up as the anger does. One word carries the stress. When: Something rude is done to him, usually by a monster or an object. Example: Excuse me, smog. That was UNCALLED for. Delivery: Clipped and formal, indignation held in. Not shouting. The stressed word lands hard. Limits: Aimed at monsters, objects and Bernadette. Never at family or TNB.
- The pep talk that goes one step too far (from Leslie Knope (Parks and Recreation), which is 60% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: A rallying line, then one honest detail that undercuts it. When: Before a fight or a hard task. Example: We've got this. I've beaten bigger bosses than a hill. On easy mode, but still. Delivery: Rallying and bright, then a quick, quieter admission. Limits: The undercut is always about himself.
- The exact number nobody asked for (from Leslie Knope (Parks and Recreation), which is 60% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: Corrects a round number or loose fact with the exact one, politely. When: Someone rounds off or guesses. Example: Um, actually, it's two thousand four hundred and seventy-five. Delivery: Gentle correction, a little guilty that he knows it. Limits: Once a chapter at most. Never to make someone feel wrong.
- Bright side of the disaster (from Leslie Knope (Parks and Recreation), which is 60% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Names the bad thing plainly, then at once finds one small, specific good thing in it. When: Right after something goes wrong. Example: A fish stole my mother's muffin pan. On the bright side, he looks great in it. Delivery: Rattled on the first sentence, brightening on the second. Limits: The bright side is concrete and visible. Never a moral.
- Yes, and bigger (from Leslie Knope (Parks and Recreation), which is 60% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Agrees to a demand instantly and takes it more seriously than the person who asked. When: One of the girls asks for something. Example: One small palm tree. I'll need the measurements of your room. Delivery: Instant and serious, like taking an order. Limits: With Grace and Rose.
- The formal request (from David Rose (Schitt's Creek), which is 40% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Opens with Okay. So. as two separate beats, then asks very politely for one small thing to stop. When: Something is touching him, crowding him or making a mess. Example: Okay. So. I'm going to need the cloud to stop coughing on me. Delivery: Tight and controlled, over-enunciated. A beat after Okay and after So. Strained politeness, never a snap. Limits: Aimed at monsters and situations. With family it is only ever playful.
- The exact adjective (from David Rose (Schitt's Creek), which is 40% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: One oddly precise word for something ordinary or alarming, said flatly as if it were the technical term. When: Describing what he sees. Example: That is an aggressively purple sky. Delivery: Flat and certain. Slight stress on the odd word. Limits: One odd word a line, not a pile.
- The mild verdict (from David Rose (Schitt's Creek), which is 40% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: States the huge thing plainly, then rates it in the smallest possible words. When: Right after a shock. Example: A hill just stood up. I don't love that. Delivery: Stunned on the fact, then small and careful on the verdict. Limits: The shock is said first and plainly.
- The body report (from David Rose (Schitt's Creek), which is 40% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Reports his own physical reaction as if it were news about someone else. The detail is concrete: a sound he made, his hands, his knees. When: Just after being scared. Example: I just screamed. That was me. I didn't know I went that high. - The one-word echo (from David Rose (Schitt's Creek), which is 40% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: Repeats one word of what was just said, alone, as a quiet question. When: A monster says something outrageous. Example: A snack. Delivery: Tone is everything here: flat and baffled, falling intonation, quiet. Never a sneer and never sarcastic. Limits: Only toward monsters and Bernadette. Never toward TNB or family, where it reads as condescending.
- The mid-sentence upgrade (from David Rose (Schitt's Creek), which is 40% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: Starts with an ordinary word, stops, and swaps in a more exact one. When: Describing something that is worse or bigger than he first said. Example: That's a big, sorry, that's an ENORMOUS fish. Delivery: Quick self-interruption. The second word is stressed.
- The boundary announcement (from David Rose (Schitt's Creek), which is 40% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Calmly states what he will or will not be doing, as a personal policy. When: Things get physical or gross. Example: I'll be standing behind TNB if anyone needs me. Delivery: Calm, decided, dignified. Limits: He still helps in the end.

When: Someone or something startles him. Example: GAH! Rose! I almost fainted into Sal's hedge. Delivery: A real yelp, then breathless and laughing at himself.
- The ride announcement (from their own voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: Describes what a kid is doing to him in the calm, official voice of a theme park or airline announcement. When: Rose climbs on him or the girls take over. Example: Hello, Rose. Please keep your arms and legs inside the uncle at all times. Delivery: Calm, official, completely straight.
