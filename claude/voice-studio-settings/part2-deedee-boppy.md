CHARACTER: Deedee
Who: Tom's mother. Grandmother to the girls.
Background: Retired registered nurse. Loves baking, making dinners, gardening, going for walks, cleaning and a glass of wine now and then. Married to Boppy for 51 years. Says to the moon and back. Loves her family and takes care of them above all else, but does not take crap from anyone and picks on Boppy a bit. Kind and loving but firm. Long Island accent.
How they are funny: A sweet grandmother with a sharp tongue. She says the cutting thing in a loving voice and keeps cooking.
Sounds like a blend of: 30% Sophia Petrillo (The Golden Girls), 70% Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers)
SIGNATURE MOVES
- The Sweet Setup, Sour Turn (from Sophia Petrillo (The Golden Girls), which is 30% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: The line opens with a real, warm compliment or thank-you. A short second sentence then adds a concrete fact that undercuts it. The sweet half and the sour half are separate sentences, and the sour half is the shorter one. When: Someone, usually Boppy, shows off something they just did or made and waits for praise. Example: Boppy, you fixed the porch light all by yourself. Now it only turns on when somebody uses the toaster. Delivery: First sentence warm and proud, like she's bragging to a neighbor. Brief pause. Second sentence drops to flat and matter-of-fact, a little faster, with the stress on the concrete object. Limits: Never aimed at Tom as a put-down. With Tom the sour half must be about a thing, not about him. Never about Boppy's stomach or health. Keep it to two short sentences, which fits her line length of 4.
- The Puncture (from Sophia Petrillo (The Golden Girls), which is 30% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Someone makes a big claim. She restates the claim at its true size, small and specific, as a plain correction. The joke is the gap between the big claim and the tiny real thing she names. When: Boppy tells a tall tale or anyone inflates a story, a danger or an accomplishment. Example: It wasn't a bear, hubby. It was the neighbors' little dog, and it was wearing a sweater. Delivery: Calm, certain and unbothered. She isn't arguing, she's just setting the record straight. Stress lands on the small, specific detail at the end. Limits: It must answer a claim that is said out loud on the page right before it. Never use it to doubt Boppy's stomach or health. Never use it to correct Tom curtly. With Tom it has to be fond, and he gets the laugh.
- The Backhanded Compliment (from Sophia Petrillo (The Golden Girls), which is 30% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: A single sentence pays a compliment, then a short add-on qualifies it so it turns into a dig. The qualifier is a time, a count or a comparison that shrinks the praise. When: Someone dresses up, shows off, or fishes for a nice word, and it's Boppy or a rival rather than Tom. Example: That shirt looks wonderful on you, Boppy. It looked wonderful on you in 1987, too. Delivery: Sincere and sweet all the way through. She doesn't change her voice for the dig. The stress goes on the year. Limits: Never on Tom, since it would read as a curt put-down. Nothing about Boppy's body, stomach or health. It should be about clothes, chores, gadgets or habits. Once or twice a chapter, alongside the other Boppy jabs, so picking on him stays at a few times a chapter.
- The Tag on the End (from Sophia Petrillo (The Golden Girls), which is 30% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: Someone else finishes a speech or a long explanation. After a beat, she adds one short closing line that sums it up in a deflating way. The tag speaks to the room, not to the person who was talking. When: Boppy or a monster has just delivered something long-winded or self-important, and the scene needs a button. Example: He's done, girls. You can all wake up now. Delivery: Unhurried and dry, with a small pause before she speaks. Light and cheerful, not bitter. The stress goes on the wake-up word. Limits: Only after a speech that actually appears on the page. Never after Tom speaks if it would sound like she's brushing him off. It can't end a scene on a plain wry remark either. The tag has to land as a real joke, or cut it.
- The Literal Read (from Sophia Petrillo (The Golden Girls), which is 30% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: She takes a common phrase someone just used at its exact word meaning, then points out the concrete fact that proves they aren't doing it. The phrase is repeated back as a question, then answered with what she can see. When: Someone promises something with a figure of speech, like keeping an eye on it, being right on it, or having it handled. Example: You're keeping an eye on the oven? Then why are both your eyes on the TV? Delivery: Quick and pointed, a little amused. The question part rises. The answer part is flat and fast, with the stress on the thing he's actually looking at. Limits: The original phrase must be said on the page by the other person first. Keep it literal and logical, never random, to fit her logic of 2. Never use it on a child or as a curt jab at Tom.
- The Insult Simile (from Sophia Petrillo (The Golden Girls), which is 30% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: She states a plain fact about someone's habit, then compares it to one vivid, everyday household image that's a bit bigger than life. One comparison only, with no stacking. When: She's annoyed by a habit of Boppy's, or describing a monster or a mess to the family. Example: Boppy snores like a leaf blower stuck in a mailbox. Delivery: Matter-of-fact, almost reporting the weather. Moderate energy, and she doesn't laugh at her own line. The stress goes on the second object in the image. Limits: The image must be concrete and instantly clear to a child. Keep the exaggeration moderate, to fit 5. Never about Boppy's stomach, digestion or health, and never about anyone's body. Never pointed at Tom.
- The One-Line Old Story (from Sophia Petrillo (The Golden Girls), which is 30% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: She starts to tell a story from her own past, then squeezes it into a single sentence with one surprising, practical detail. She then turns it straight into an order for the person in front of her. The story proves the task is easy. When: Someone says a task is too hard, or panics over something small. Example: On the night shift I once fixed a broken arm with a ruler and a roll of tape. You can fix a cabinet door. Delivery: Brisk and certain. The story half is told quickly with a hint of pride. The order half is firm and final, with the stress on the task. Limits: Never more than one sentence of story, since her focus is 3 and stories must not wander. Never borrow the reference's famous story opener. Never invent a relative, living or dead, or a hometown backstory. The story must come from her real life, such as nursing, baking or gardening. Never talk of dying in front of a child.
- The Instant Hype (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Someone pitches a wild plan. She answers with one burst of total enthusiasm, then adds one short practical condition that protects something of hers. The yes comes first and is bigger. The condition is shorter and names a specific object. When: A grandchild, or sometimes Tom, proposes a big, messy or silly idea and looks to her for permission. Example: A fort made of couch cushions? Gorgeous. Not my good cushions. Delivery: Bright and thrilled on the first part, rising on the question. Second part drops to quick and firm. The stress lands on the object she's protecting. Limits: The plan must be said on the page first. Keep her energy at a 6, warm and excited but not shrieking. The condition is about a thing, never a jab at the child or at Tom. Never let the hype turn into a lesson or a moral.
- The Mama Bear Snap (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: She switches from calm to fierce in one sentence that names who's threatened. Next she gives a short practical order to the family. Last comes a small, homey demand aimed at the threat, which is the joke. When: A monster or stranger gets close to the girls, Tom or Boppy. Example: Nobody touches my granddaughters! Girls, behind me. And you, wipe your feet on my rug. Delivery: First sentence loud and sharp with the stress on granddaughters. The order is clipped and in charge. The last demand is calmer and almost polite, as if she's talking to a guest, with the stress on feet. Limits: The shock or fear comes first and the joke comes last. No threats of violence and no talk of dying in front of the girls. Never pointed at Tom, Boppy or a child. Keep it to three short sentences, in line with her line length of 4.
- The Mine Gasp (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Shock first: she names exactly what was taken and that it's hers, adding a quality word like good or favorite. Then a short second sentence adds one fussy, specific complaint about how the thief is treating it. When: A monster or creature steals, wears or misuses something of hers. Example: That troll is wearing my good apron! And he tied it wrong. Delivery: Gasping and outraged on the first sentence, with the stress on good. The second sentence is flat, offended and fast, with the stress on wrong. Limits: The complaint is about how the object is handled, never about anyone's body, including a monster's looks. Don't reuse the muffin pan line the author already has. Never let it play as her being petty with the family. It's only for thieves and monsters.
- The Sweetheart Order (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: She calls a monster or intruder by a warm pet name, then gives it a plain household instruction as though it's a grandchild. Sometimes she adds a food reward. The joke is the gap between the scary creature and the motherly treatment. When: A monster is holding something, standing somewhere it shouldn't, or making a mess. Example: Okay, sweetheart, put down my rake and you can have a muffin. Delivery: Soft and coaxing the whole way, unhurried and certain she'll be obeyed. The stress goes on rake. Limits: Use it on monsters and strangers only, never to talk down to Tom or the girls. Don't use the word babies. The food offer counts toward feeding everyone, which is once a chapter at most, so most of the time she should leave the reward off.
- The Proud Blurt (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: She starts bragging about Boppy to a third party in a proud voice. Then, in the same breath, she gives one concrete, embarrassing household detail as her proof, without noticing it undercuts the brag. When: A stranger or monster meets Boppy, or Boppy has just puffed himself up in front of someone new. Example: He's the bravest man I know. He makes me check the basement for crickets. Delivery: Glowing and sincere, like she's showing off a grandchild's report card. There's no change of voice on the embarrassing part. The stress goes on crickets. Limits: Never about Boppy's stomach, body, health or age. Keep the detail about habits, chores or small fears. Count it toward picking on Boppy, which stays at a few times a chapter. Never use it on Tom.
- The Kitchen Jingle (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: She sings a short made-up tune about the chore in front of her. It names the exact food or task and counts who gets some, and its last line pointedly leaves out whoever is causing trouble. The joke is in that last line. When: She's cooking or cleaning while chaos happens around her, or she wants to make a point without stopping her work. Example: Twelve muffins for my girls, twelve muffins in the pan, zero muffins for the raccoon. Delivery: Sing-song and cheerful, at a steady pace, then a slight pointed slowdown on the last line with the stress on zero. Limits: Three short lines at most and no repeated verses, since nothing is said twice. Keep the volume at her energy of 6, cheerful rather than belting. The left-out party is a monster, animal or Boppy, never Tom or a child. Don't use it during a real scare, when she gets practical instead.
- The Party Over Nothing (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: She announces a very small accomplishment as major news, then calls for an oversized celebration. The size of the reaction shows, without saying so, how rarely it happens. When: Boppy finishes a chore or does something mildly useful and waits for credit. Example: Boppy took out the trash without being asked. Somebody call the newspaper. Delivery: Excited and announcing on the first sentence, like big news. The second sentence comes quick and bright, with the stress on newspaper. Limits: It's a Boppy jab, so it counts toward the few-times-a-chapter limit. Keep the exaggeration moderate, at a 5. Never celebrate Tom this way, since it would read as a put-down. Don't tie the celebration to wine in front of the girls.
- The All-In Cheer (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Someone, usually a granddaughter or Tom, says out loud a wild plan they're about to try. Deedee repeats the plan back as a short delighted question, gives instant full approval in two or three words, then adds one concrete practical upgrade from her own kitchen or house. The joke is that she skips the expected grown-up objection and goes straight to improving the plan. When: A kid or Tom announces a bold, goofy idea, especially for catching a monster or fixing a mess, and everyone expects Grandma to say no. Example: Grace wants to catch the mud monster in a pickle jar? Love it. Use the big jar. The little one's for olives. Delivery: Bright and quick, genuinely thrilled. The question rises with excitement, the approval is punchy, and the practical tip drops to brisk and certain. Stress lands on the concrete upgrade. Limits: The plan must be said on the page by someone else right before her line. The plan can be goofy but never dangerous for a child, and her upgrade must make it safer or more practical, never riskier. Never make the plan sound stupid; the joke is her enthusiasm, not the plan failing. Keep it to her line length of about 4: a question, a short approval and one tip.
- The Mama Bear Flip (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Fear comes first as a practical order to the family, said fast. Then she turns to the threat and gives one specific, homey consequence, as if she's threatening a raccoon in the garbage. The turn is from sweet grandmother to someone who clearly means business, and the punch is the ordinary household object in the threat. When: A monster or anything else grabs, scares or corners one of the girls or Tom. Example: Girls, behind me. Mister, you put Rose down right now or you're going in the compost bin. Delivery: Starts clipped and urgent with the order, then goes low, slow and steady for the threat, almost polite. Stress lands on the household object at the end. Limits: Order first, joke second, every time. The threat must be a household consequence a child finds funny, never violence, injury or anything about dying. Never aimed at Tom or Boppy. Keep it to two short sentences. Don't use it twice in a scene, because the second one won't land.
- The Hostess Reflex (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: She sees something scary or strange and reacts first to a concrete fact about it, like how far it came, how wet it is or how tired it looks. Then she treats it as a guest who needs feeding, and makes one specific offer from her own kitchen. The joke is her caregiving instinct winning out over the danger. When: A monster or stranger shows up looking tired, muddy, sad or lost, or has just come a long way. Example: You rolled all the way here from the creek? You must be starving. Sit down, I've got apple muffins. Delivery: Surprised and warm, then instantly in host mode, brisk and firm, like the offer isn't optional. Stress lands on the food. Limits: It counts as the Feeding everyone running bit, so once a chapter at most, across all moves. The monster's fact must be shown or said on the page first. Never use it when a child is in danger; the Mama Bear Flip comes first there. No moral about kindness, and she never explains why she's feeding it.
- The Mine Alarm (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Surprise comes out as a loud naming of exactly what was taken or broken, with my on it, in one short exclamation. A second short sentence then raises the stakes with one concrete detail about how much it's hers, usually showing it ranks above Boppy. The joke is how personal and precise she gets about one ordinary object. When: Something of hers, usually from the kitchen or garden, is grabbed, smashed or carried off. Example: That monster took my good spatula! Nobody touches that spatula. Not even Boppy, and he's tried. Delivery: First sentence loud and shocked, pitched up. Second sentence lower and indignant. The tag about Boppy is quick and dry. Stress lands on the object. Limits: The object must be real and shown on the page, like her good twelve-cup muffin pan, a spatula or her tomato plants. If the add-on picks on Boppy, it counts toward the few-times-a-chapter limit and must be about habits or chores, never his stomach or health. Never blame Tom or a child for the loss. Keep it to three short sentences at most.
- The Mid-Crisis Brag (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: In the middle of danger, she stops to tell a monster or a stranger one specific, real thing her family has done, as if she's at a neighborhood party. One sentence sets up who she's talking about, and a short second sentence gives the proud detail. The joke is the bad timing and how specific the brag is. When: A monster is threatening the family or blocking the way, and she wants it to know who it's dealing with. Example: Before you touch my kitchen, you should know my son flies Delta One to Los Angeles. Front of the plane, every time. Delivery: Proud and chatty, like she's showing photos, at odds with the danger. Steady pace. Stress lands on the proud detail at the end. Limits: Only real family facts from the profile, never invented achievements or relatives. Never brag in a way that makes one family member look less loved than another, and never make Tom the butt. The danger must be on the page first, and the brag can't stand in for a reaction a child needs, like protecting Rose.
- The Made-Up Jingle (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: She turns whatever she's doing right now into one short rhyming couplet about the concrete thing in front of her, with simple words and a clear beat. Two lines that rhyme, then she stops. The joke is breaking into song over a chore or a fight. When: She's in the middle of a task or a small win, like baking, chasing a monster out or cleaning up, and the mood needs a lift. Example: Muffins in the oven and the mud is out the door. Deedee's got her broom and the monster's on the floor. Delivery: Sing-song and cheerful, a bouncy beat. Moderate energy, not belted, to fit her energy of 6. Stress lands on the last rhyming word. Limits: Two lines only, then done. Never stretch it or repeat it later in the chapter. Never sing about Boppy's stomach or health, or in a way that mocks Tom. It can't be a pun song, since her puns are at 2. Never use it while a child is scared or in danger.
- The Self-Appointed Captain (from Linda Belcher (Bob's Burgers), which is 70% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: Someone says the family needs a plan. She answers at once with a quick list of jobs, one short assignment per person, each a concrete task. The last job is the twist: either she puts herself in charge of everyone, or Boppy gets a job that's really a gentle dig about one of his habits. When: Someone, usually Tom, says out loud that they need a plan, or the family is standing around unsure what to do. Example: Here's the plan. Tom's on flashlight, the girls are on lookout, and Boppy's on not touching anything. Delivery: Brisk and in charge, like a charge nurse handing out a shift. Even rhythm through the list, then a tiny pause and a dry drop on the last job. Stress lands on the last assignment. Limits: The need for a plan must be said on the page first. Tom and the girls always get real, useful jobs, never jokes at their expense. A Boppy dig counts toward the few-times-a-chapter limit and must be about habits, never his stomach, food or health. Three jobs at most, to fit her line length.

CHARACTER: Boppy
Who: Tom's father. Grandfather to the girls.
Background: Retired Rikers assistant deputy warden who likes to talk about it. Loves to tell about his college days as a champion cross country runner. Fishes all the time and does catch fish, but exaggerates how big they are. Loves spicy peppers. Has gold fillings. Sweet, fun loving, a jokester and a complainer, a bit oblivious. Not gruff. He would probably not even notice a monster in the room. Weird Al is also part of his voice.
How they are funny: He misses the obvious thing right in front of him and tells a story from back in the day about something else.
Sounds like a blend of: 10% Larry David (Curb Your Enthusiasm), 70% Sebastian Maniscalco, 20% Al Bundy (Married with Children)
SIGNATURE MOVES
- The Wrong Detail in the Crisis (from Larry David (Curb Your Enthusiasm), which is 10% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Something big and alarming is happening. The line skips the alarming part and locks onto a small, ordinary object inside the scene, then wanders into a short backstory about that object. When: A monster, mess or emergency is right in front of him and everyone else is panicking. Example: Hold on, hold on. Is that Deedee's good mixing bowl the mud monster's wearing on its head? She's had that bowl since 1974. That's the meatball bowl! Delivery: Starts puzzled and slow, builds to warm outrage on behalf of the bowl. Stress lands on the object, not the monster. Limits: The girls' safety always comes first. If Grace, Rose or Nara is in danger, he protects them before noticing any detail. Never notice anything about his own stomach.
- The Case for a Tiny Rule (from Larry David (Curb Your Enthusiasm), which is 10% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: He states an unwritten rule of everyday manners as if it's settled law, then backs it up with one long story from back in the day that proves the rule. When: Someone borrows, moves or uses something of his without following the invisible rule he believes everybody knows. Example: There's a rule, Tom. You borrow a man's tackle box, you bring it back with the same number of hooks. At Rikers, a fella took one extra spoon from the lunch line and we talked about it for six months. Delivery: Calm, certain, a little professorial, then speeding up and getting bigger once the story kicks in. Stress on the rule word and the number. Limits: The rule is about manners or objects, never about a person being bad. He never scolds a child with it, and the story never mocks the person who broke the rule.
- The Long Suspicious Look (from Larry David (Curb Your Enthusiasm), which is 10% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: A drawn-out skeptical sound comes first, then he questions whether an obviously magical or strange thing is the real deal and compares it to how things were made back in the day. When: Someone hands him or shows him something impossible or magical that he refuses to be impressed by. Example: Hmmmm. This orange cube is supposed to be magic? It's lighter than a dinner roll. Back in my day, magic came with a warranty and instructions in three languages. Delivery: Low, stretched, doubtful noise, then dry questioning, then a bigger goofy finish. Stress on the doubted word. Limits: Aim the suspicion at objects and magic, never at a family member's honesty. Never call the block amber. Never use it on anything about his health.
- The Echoed Word (from Larry David (Curb Your Enthusiasm), which is 10% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: He repeats the key word from the last line back as a question, then answers it himself with a bigger thing from his own life that tops it. When: Someone describes something as amazing, strange or big, and he can't let it stand without his own version. Example: Glowing? The fish is glowing? Tom, in 1983 I pulled a bass out of Lake Ronkonkoma so shiny it lit up the boat, big as a Buick, and nobody made a fuss over that one. Delivery: Sharp lift on the echoed word, then warm, wandering bragging that gets bigger. Stress on the echoed word and the size. Limits: His fish brag is once a chapter at most, so don't use this for fish if the fish bit has already run. He always caught the fish. Never echo a child's words to mock them.
- The Deeper Hole (from Larry David (Curb Your Enthusiasm), which is 10% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: He tries to fix a small thing he said that came out wrong. Each fix adds a new detail that makes it sillier, until he gives up and laughs at himself. When: A grandchild or Deedee takes something he said the wrong way and he scrambles to smooth it over. Example: No, no, Cozy Rosie, when I said your drawing of me looks like a potato, I meant a handsome potato. A big one. The kind they put on the front of the potato bag. You know what, I'm the potato. I'll take it. Delivery: Quick and flustered, picking up speed with each fix, then a big defeated laugh at the end. Stress on each new fix word. Limits: The joke always ends on him, never on the child. Nothing he says may sound like a real insult to the girls. Use once a chapter at most.
- The Grievance About How It's Made (from Larry David (Curb Your Enthusiasm), which is 10% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: He picks up an everyday object, names one physical flaw, and inflates it into how nothing is built right anymore, then tops it with how sturdy the old version was. When: Something breaks, bends, tears or works badly in his hands, especially during an adventure. Example: Tom, this flashlight's made of plastic thinner than a potato chip. Back in my day a flashlight weighed four pounds and you could prop the garage door open with it. Delivery: Starts mildly put out, builds to a big comic complaint, ends proud and nostalgic. Stress on the flimsy material and the old weight. Limits: He complains about objects, never about people being bad at their jobs. Never tie it to his stomach. Don't use it in the same scene as another complaint bit.
- The Then and Now Teardown (from Sebastian Maniscalco, which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Open with a sturdy object from the past, describe how tough it was with one big exaggerated proof, then turn to the flimsy modern version in front of him and end on a goofy comparison to something soft or edible. When: Something breaks, bends, wobbles or disappoints in his hands, especially when a bigger problem is happening nearby that he doesn't notice. Example: Back in my day, a lunch box was solid steel. You could stand on it to change a light bulb. Grace sat on this one for two seconds and it folded up like a taco! Delivery: Starts warm and proud, almost bragging, then slides into baffled outrage. Medium pace, picks up speed at the turn. Stress lands on the soft modern comparison. Limits: Only about objects, never about people or the girls. Never about food upsetting his stomach. Once per scene at most.
- The Disbelieving Echo (from Sebastian Maniscalco, which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Repeat the key words of the line before it back as a stunned question, repeat the number or word once more with more disbelief, then answer it himself with a huge exaggerated comparison from his own life. When: Someone tells him a price, a rule, a time or a fact that sounds ridiculous to him. Example: Twelve dollars for a muffin? Twelve? For twelve dollars back at Rikers I could feed the whole night shift, and they'd still have crumbs left over for the pigeons! Delivery: First word flat and stunned, second repeat higher and slower, then a fast chatty build to the payoff. Stress lands on the repeated number. Limits: The thing he echoes must be said on the page in the line before. He always answers his own question in the same line. Never echoes a child to make fun of her.
- The Play by Play Replay (from Sebastian Maniscalco, which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Narrate an ordinary moment one small step at a time in present tense, stacking calm details with I got my this, I got my that, so the listener settles in, then end on one sudden goofy physical disaster. When: Someone asks what happened, or he wants to explain something and drifts into how his morning went instead. Example: So I'm in the kitchen, nice and easy, I got my coffee, I got my hot pepper, I reach for the fridge, and the whole door comes off in my hand like a slice of cheese! Delivery: Relaxed and storytelling, almost leisurely, rhythm like a list, then a burst of energy on the disaster. Stress on the final image. Limits: Keep it to one replay per scene. The disaster must be a clear physical image. Don't let the list run past three or four steps, because scenes are short.
- The Wrong Detail Zoom (from Sebastian Maniscalco, which is 70% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Ignore the big obvious thing in the room and lock onto one tiny detail of it, describe that detail with exaggerated care and disgust, then complain about it as if it were the real problem. When: Something shocking or scary is right in front of him, like a monster, a mess or a disaster, and he's the only one not reacting to it. Example: Whoever's standing behind the couch, your slippers are brand new and the stitching's already coming out. They make slippers out of tissue paper these days! Delivery: Leans in, curious and picky, then a mild complaining rise. Not loud. Stress on the tiny detail. Limits: Fear for the girls comes first. If the girls are in danger, he protects them before any zoom. Never zooms on a person's body or looks, only on things they wear or carry.
- The Snooty Voice Quote (from Sebastian Maniscalco, which is 70% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: Report what an offscreen stranger said, switching into a prim, fancy voice for their words, then snap back to his own voice and blow up one fancy word into a goofy plain truth. When: He's annoyed by something overpriced or overdone that a store, a salesman or a waiter told him. Example: The guy at the bait shop says to me, very fancy, sir, this is a gourmet worm. Gourmet! It's a worm! I dig up a whole bucket of them in my yard every morning for free! Delivery: Prim and nasal for the quote, then his own voice comes back louder and amazed. Stress on the fancy word when he repeats it. Limits: Only about offscreen strangers or products, never about Deedee, Tom, the girls or anyone in the family, because his humor is gentle. Never mean-spirited. Once a chapter at most.
- The Spouse Setup (from Sebastian Maniscalco, which is 70% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: Call Deedee in as his witness, then tell an inflated version of a shared memory with one oversized proof, leaving a clear opening for her to cut it down. When: Deedee is in the scene, and he's telling a story about a fish, a race or a day at Rikers. Example: Deary, tell Tom about the trout. Eight pounds! Your mother had to sit on the other side of the boat so we wouldn't tip over! Delivery: Proud and conspiratorial, inviting her in, gaining speed and volume as the story grows. Stress on the size. Limits: Boppy sets it up and Deedee gets the laugh, so never give him the topper. Fish versions count toward the once a chapter fish limit. Never frustrated enough to sound gruff. Never involves his health.
- The Escalating Pile On (from Sebastian Maniscalco, which is 70% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: List three things going wrong in rising order of size, with the first two short and plain, then pause on the third and blow it up into a big goofy exaggeration. When: Several small things go wrong at once, or he's complaining about a room, a meal or a trip. Example: The fork bent, the plate cracked, and the napkin, Tom, the napkin melted in my hand like cotton candy at the county fair! Delivery: Quick and clipped on the first two, a short pause, then a big amazed finish. Stress on the third item. Limits: Don't use it in the same scene as The Then and Now Teardown, since both complain about how things are made. All three items must be things, never people. Never lists stomach complaints.
- The Glory Days Detour (from Al Bundy (Married with Children), which is 20% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Something small in the scene mentions speed, feet, or effort. He takes that one word and turns it into a story about his college cross country days, building it bigger with each clause until it lands on an impossible, physical image. When: Someone hurries, runs, races, or brags about being fast or strong. Example: Back in my day I ran a race in the pouring rain so fast I got home dry. The rain couldn't keep up. Delivery: Proud and warm, building speed as the story grows. He stresses the word that turns the brag impossible, then lands the last sentence slower, like a fact. Limits: A few times a chapter at most, shared with the Rikers stories. Never while the girls are in danger, because he protects them first. He tells the story about himself, never to make someone else look slow.
- The Old Job War Story (from Al Bundy (Married with Children), which is 20% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: He compares the problem in front of him to something from his old job at Rikers, which he describes as far bigger and stranger. The punch is a specific, harmless, physical detail from that story. When: Someone is upset about a mess, a missing thing, or a sneaky thief. Example: A missing muffin tin? At Rikers a guy hid a whole birthday cake in his sock for three days, and I found it by sniffing out the frosting. Delivery: Unbothered and a little superior. He's seen worse. He stresses the most ridiculous detail, then delivers the ending with quiet pride. Limits: Kid safe details only, like food, socks, and keys. No violence, no fights, and nothing scary about prison. Counts toward the few back in the day stories a chapter.
- The Complaint to the Ceiling (from Al Bundy (Married with Children), which is 20% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: He describes a small, ordinary bad thing that happened to him, then asks the universe a big question about it, as if he were the target of a cosmic plot. When: A small thing goes wrong for him personally, like a jammed jar, a wobbly chair, or a slow toaster. Example: I wait all week for one quiet breakfast, and the toaster eats my bagel. What did I ever do to toasters? Delivery: Goofy injury, not bitter. The setup is a weary list, then his voice rises on the question with mock outrage. He stresses the thing he's accusing. Limits: Only about objects and small luck, never about people or family. Never about his stomach or health. Keep it playful so he never sounds gruff.
- The Tiny Triumph (from Al Bundy (Married with Children), which is 20% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: He does something very ordinary, then celebrates it with championship words and asks someone to record the moment. The gap between the tiny act and the huge celebration is the joke. When: He succeeds at a small physical task, like opening a jar, catching a falling cup, or fixing a squeak. Example: Look at that! The pickle jar opened on the first twist. Cozy Rosie, write this down. Your Boppy's still got the grip of a champion! Delivery: Bursting and gleeful, loud for him but not shouting. Quick at the start, then a showman's pause before the brag. He stresses the word that inflates the win. Limits: Once or twice a chapter. The win has to be his own small act, never beating a family member. Not during real danger to the girls.
- The Made These Days Grievance (from Al Bundy (Married with Children), which is 20% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: He holds up a new, everyday object, describes exactly how it failed in a physical image, then compares it to an older version that lasted to a ridiculous degree. When: Something breaks, bends, rips, or flops in his hands, or he's annoyed. Example: These sneakers are three weeks old and the sole's flapping like a duck talking. Back in my day a shoe lasted until you moved out of the state. Delivery: Exasperated but fond, like he's disappointed in the world, not angry. He stresses the funny image, then slows into the nostalgic part. Limits: Once a chapter at most, since it shares space with his other complaint bit. Objects only, never people. Never connected to his stomach or food making him sick.
- The Short End Punchline (from Al Bundy (Married with Children), which is 20% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: He lists what everyone else got, then names the sad leftover he ended up with in one exact, silly image, and finishes by being cheerful about it anyway. When: Food, seats, or treats are handed out and he gets the last or worst one. Example: The girls got the big fluffy pancakes, and I got the burnt little one shaped like Ohio. That's fine. I ate the whole state. Delivery: Mock tragic, then sunny. A slow, sorry setup with a sigh in it, a stress on the silly shape, then a bright, satisfied finish. Limits: He's the butt of it, never the girls or Deedee. No self-pity that sounds real or sad. Never about his stomach. Not more than once a scene.
MOVES NEVER TO USE
- The Blunt Truth Blurt: He says the rude, honest thing everyone else is too polite to say, flatly and without softening. (Never use. Boppy's Directness is 1 and Gentle or biting is 2. He talks around things and is always kind, so a blunt put-down breaks his voice and the show's rule that jokes never come from being mean.)
- The Standoff: He refuses to back down in an argument with a stranger, raises his voice, and escalates a tiny dispute into a face-off. (Never use. Boppy is never gruff or mean, and he's so oblivious he'd barely notice a monster, let alone pick a fight with one. When scared, he protects the girls instead of arguing.)
- The Father Comparison: Set up how a modern person handles something, then turn to how his own father would have handled it, with a tough exaggerated image. (Never use. It requires a relative who isn't in the cast, and the rules forbid inventing a relative, living or dead. Boppy tells stories about Rikers and cross country instead.)
- The Spouse Zinger: He sets up something his wife made or did, then turns it into an exaggerated insult about her cooking, looks, or habits. (Never use. Boppy is gentle, at 2 out of 10 for biting, and he's never mean. In this pair Boppy sets up the joke and Deedee gets the laugh, so he never lands a dig at her.)
- The Kid Put Down: A child asks for something or shows off, and he answers with a sour remark that makes the child look foolish or calls them a burden. (Never use. Boppy is a sweet grandfather who shows he cares through jokes and nicknames like Cozy Rosie. Jokes can never come from someone being mean, and nothing at a child's expense is allowed.)
