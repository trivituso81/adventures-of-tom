Background: Intensely commanding and snippy, with an attitude problem and a chip on her shoulder. Calls Tom 3468, his house number. Delivers relentless, dry digs at people's clothes and status. Tom and TNB are always slightly annoyed with her and roll their eyes a bit.
How they are funny: Devastating insults delivered as if they were neighborhood announcements.
Sounds like a blend of: 40% Lucille Bluth (Arrested Development), 60% Karen Walker (Will and Grace)
SIGNATURE MOVES
- The Backhanded Compliment (from Lucille Bluth (Arrested Development), which is 40% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Open with sincere-sounding praise of one specific thing, then turn it in the final clause so the praise becomes the insult. The setup is polite and the turn is a plain, concrete comparison. When: Someone shows her something new, wears something new, or seems proud of something. Example: That's a lovely shirt, 3468. It's very brave to wear a tablecloth in public. Delivery: Smooth and gracious on the praise, then flat and cool on the turn. Even pace. Stress lands on the word that flips it. Limits: Never about anyone's weight or body. Aim it at clothes, cars, houses and status. Don't stack two compliments before the turn.
- The Mock Concern (from Lucille Bluth (Arrested Development), which is 40% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Frame the insult as worry for the other person's wellbeing. State the concern first, then name the specific flaw as if it were a symptom she's been bravely noticing. When: Someone looks rumpled, tired, underdressed or out of place. Example: I'm worried about you, 3468. A grown man shouldn't have to get dressed in the dark every morning. Delivery: Low, measured, faintly pitying. Slow on the concern, slightly crisper on the flaw. Stress on the worry word. Limits: Never about health, the body or weight. Never let it slide into real warmth. It must stay an insult.
- The Exit Cut (from Lucille Bluth (Arrested Development), which is 40% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: A short reason for leaving that's actually the insult. Structure: I'd do the polite thing, but your specific item is causing a specific harm. When: She's done with a conversation, or wants the last word. Example: I'd stay and chat, 3468, but your sandals are lowering my property value. Delivery: Polite and unhurried on the setup, then clipped and final on the reason. Stress on the damaged thing. Limits: Only at the end of a scene or exchange. Never more than once a scene, and never in the middle of a beat that needs an answer.
- The Instant Blame Flip (from Lucille Bluth (Arrested Development), which is 40% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: React to the alarming thing in the first clause, then immediately pin it on the nearest person and back it with a specific Neighborhood Watch rule. Fear comes first, the blame is the joke. When: Something startles or scares her, or something goes wrong near her. Example: There's a raccoon in your trash can, 3468. Rule four. Lids stay latched. Clearly the raccoon read the rules and you didn't. Delivery: Sharp, offended jolt on the first line, then cold and procedural. Stress on the rule number and on you. Limits: Neighborhood Watch rules once a chapter at most, so the rule version is rare. Never make Tom look stupid or incompetent. The joke is her reflex, not his failure.
- The Price Tag Blindness (from Lucille Bluth (Arrested Development), which is 40% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: Treat an ordinary price or everyday purchase as shockingly low, as if she's never encountered anything so cheap. The question sounds sincere and the turn is her suspicion of it. When: Someone mentions what something cost, or shows her a bargain. Example: Fourteen dollars for a sandwich? I didn't know they made sandwiches that cheap. Is it safe? Delivery: Genuinely puzzled, quiet, slightly squeamish. Stress on cheap. Limits: Keep it about status, never about anyone being poor in a way that's mean. Once a chapter is plenty.
- The Public Ranking (from Lucille Bluth (Arrested Development), which is 40% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: Announce a ranking of people or things as if it's settled fact, place the target last, then add one concrete item that it lost to. When: Anyone asks her opinion, or she wants to put someone in their place. Example: Of all the houses on this street, 3468, yours is my least favorite. And I'm including the empty lot. Delivery: Calm, official, like reading results. Stress on least, then a dry pause before the topper. Limits: Never rank Tom's family members against each other, and never rank children. Neighbors, houses, cars and outfits only.
- The Manners Correction (from Lucille Bluth (Arrested Development), which is 40% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: Correct a casual word or gesture someone just used, give the proper version, then explain where the casual version belongs with a crisp, insulting comparison. When: Someone greets her casually, uses slang, or skips a formality. Example: It's good evening, 3468. Hey is what you say to a horse. Delivery: Precise and frosty. Each word placed. Stress on the proper phrase, then flat on the comparison. Limits: Only right after someone actually says the casual word on the page. Never correct a child.
- The Backhanded Compliment (from Karen Walker (Will and Grace), which is 60% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Open with real-sounding praise for one specific thing, then add a final clause that reveals the praise was an insult all along. The sting always sits in the last few words, never earlier. When: Someone shows off something new or is visibly proud of their house, yard, outfit or cooking. Example: 3468, your lawn looks lovely this week. Whoever you finally paid to mow it should be very proud. Delivery: Pleasant and unhurried at first, then the turn lands flat and cool with no change in volume. Stress falls on the word that flips the meaning. Limits: Never praise or insult anyone's weight or body shape. The praise must never sound sincerely warm on its own. Don't stack two in one scene.
- The Poverty Bafflement (from Karen Walker (Will and Grace), which is 60% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Notice an ordinary, everyday object or habit, then react as though it's a strange relic she's only heard about. It's a short question, followed by a calm, superior remark that treats the thing as foreign to her world. When: Someone uses, wears or carries something cheap, practical or homemade. Example: Is that a paper coupon in your hand, 3468? How quaint. I've only seen those behind glass in a museum. Delivery: Mildly curious, almost polite, never loud. The disdain comes through in how slowly and calmly she says it. Stress the plain object word. Limits: Mock the object, never the person's intelligence or ability. Never use it on the children. Keep it to one clear object everyone recognizes.
- The Lowbrow Comparison (from Karen Walker (Will and Grace), which is 60% of the voice; use: Often). Built like this: Name a specific item of clothing or possession, then compare it in exact, concrete detail to something cheap, tacky or humble. The comparison is precise, never vague, so the image is instantly clear. When: Someone walks into her view wearing or holding something she disapproves of. Example: Attention, 3468. Your shorts are the exact color and shine of a gas station hot dog. Delivery: Calm, official and matter-of-fact, like a neighborhood bulletin. The comparison is said with complete certainty. Stress the cheap object. Limits: Clothes, cars, houses and objects only. Never compare a person's body, size or face to anything. Never anything a child shouldn't read.
- The Name Demotion (from Karen Walker (Will and Grace), which is 60% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: Refuse to use the person's real name. Refer to them by a label based on their address, an item they're wearing or their status, then carry on as if that's simply what they're called. When: Addressing or mentioning anyone she considers beneath her, which is everyone. Example: The gentleman in the rubber sandals will kindly remove his recycling bin from the curb by noon. Delivery: Formal, crisp and detached, like reading from a notice. Stress the demoting label. Limits: Use 3468 for Tom. Don't invent new labels for the family's real children. Don't use more than one new label per scene.
- The Mock Condolence (from Karen Walker (Will and Grace), which is 60% of the voice; use: Sometimes). Built like this: Express sympathy in formal, sorrowful words, but aim it at a status failure such as bad clothes or a cheap car. The sympathy is the setup. The thing she's pitying is the punchline. When: Someone tells her about a trip, a purchase or something they're excited about. Example: Oh, 3468. You fly all the way in from Long Island and you still land wearing that jacket. My sympathies to the airline. Delivery: Low, slow and falsely gentle, then cool on the final words. Stress the item being pitied. Limits: Never about illness, Boppy's health, injury or death. Only clothes, possessions and status. Never sound genuinely sorry.
- The Staff Name-Drop (from Karen Walker (Will and Grace), which is 60% of the voice; use: Rarely). Built like this: Mention her own household help as if everyone has them, and use them as the measuring stick that the other person falls short of. When: Someone shows off a possession or a skill she wants to outrank. Example: My housekeeper drives a nicer car than yours, 3468, and she's asked me not to park it near you. Delivery: Breezy and offhand, as if it's obvious. The final clause is drier and slower. Stress the comparison word. Limits: Only unnamed staff, never a relative. Never mock the staff themselves. At most once a chapter.
